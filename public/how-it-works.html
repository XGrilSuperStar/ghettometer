How the Ghettometer score works

Twelve measurable factors, public data, and a simple rule: compare every neighborhood to the rest of the country.

The short version

The map is divided into neighborhoods based on U.S. Census tracts. For each one, Ghettometer measures twelve factors. Some are bad signs (more of them means a worse score) and some are good signs (more of them means a better score). The factors are combined into one number, then every neighborhood is ranked against all the others. A score of 20 means the area looks worse than about 20 percent of neighborhoods in the dataset; a score near 0 means it is among the nicest.

Data sources

  • U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey: income and education.
  • HUD (U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development): public housing locations.
  • Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI): home value trends.
  • Overture Maps Foundation: locations of restaurants, stores, parks, transit stops and other places.
  • Public school test score data: reading and math results.
  • USGS The National Map: topographic and satellite base maps (public domain).
  • OpenStreetMap, OpenFreeMap and OpenMapTiles: the 3D building view.

Choosing which factors count

On the Layers panel you can switch each factor on or off for scoring. If you do not care about transit because you drive everywhere, turn it off and the score recalculates instantly. A separate "Map" checkbox for each place type shows the actual locations as colored dots, so you can see them whether or not they are in the score.

The pin and the half-mile circle

When you click the map, a pin drops and a circle shows a half-mile radius. The counts under the dial (how many fast food places, parks, and so on) are for places inside that circle. Income, education and home value figures describe the surrounding neighborhood, because the Census publishes those only at the neighborhood level. The score dots use a one-mile radius from the center of each neighborhood.

Limits you should know about

  • Business listings change constantly. A place may have opened or closed since the data was collected.
  • Census figures are survey estimates with a margin of error, especially in small neighborhoods.
  • The score does not include crime data, noise, traffic, flood risk or the condition of individual buildings.
  • Counting a business type says nothing about the quality of any single business.
  • Rankings are relative. A neighborhood can score "worse" than average and still be a good place to live.

Corrections

If you see wrong data for a place you know, tell us on the Contact page. Include the address and what is wrong.
